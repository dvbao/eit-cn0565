Author Manuscript
                    Abstract
                         Introduction—We determined the reliability and validity of electrical impedance myography
                         (EIM) in facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD).

                         Methods—We performed a prospective study of EIM on 16 bilateral limb and trunk muscles in
                         35 genetically defined and clinically affected FSHD patients (reliability testing on n=18).
                         Summary scores based on body region were derived. Reactance and phase (50 and 100 kHz) were
                         compared to measures of strength, FSHD disease severity, and functional outcomes.

                         Results—Participants were mostly men, mean age 53.0 years, and included a full range of
                         severity. Limb and trunk muscles showed good to excellent reliability [intraclass correlation
                         coefficients (ICC) 0.72-0.99]. Summary scores for the arm, leg, and trunk showed excellent




Author Manuscript
                         reliability (ICC 0.89-0.98). Reactance was the most sensitive EIM parameter to a broad range of
                         FSHD disease metrics.

                         Discussion—EIM is a reliable measure of muscle composition in FSHD which offers the
                         possibility to serially evaluate affected muscles.

                    Keywords
                         Muscular dystrophy; Facioscapulohumeral Muscular Dystrophy; Electrical Impedance
                         Myography; Muscle Composition; Outcome Measures

                        Introduction




Author Manuscript
                                       Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) is among the most common muscular
                                       dystrophies and causes progressive loss of motor function. Approximately 20% of people
                                       require a wheelchair over age 50.1,2 FSHD is notable for marked side-to-side asymmetry.
                                       Although considerable variability exists between patients, there is a characteristic pattern of
                                       progression involving the muscles of the face and shoulders, then later distal lower
                                       extremity, and finally the hip girdle muscles. In addition, patients may have debilitating
                                       involvement of abdominal and paraspinal muscles.3,4 Although the individual experience of
                                       the disease may suggest periods of rapid worsening followed by periods of stability, the
                                       largest natural history study to date showed a slow but progressive loss of strength over
                                       time.5,6 Recent advances in our molecular understanding of FSHD have identified potential
                                       therapeutic targets for future clinical trials, and the focus of the research community is
                                       shifting towards clinical trial planning.7 Traditional measures of strength testing would




Author Manuscript
                                       require long follow up periods or large numbers of participants to demonstrate halt of
                                       progression, but more importantly there are not well validated techniques for quantifying
                                       changes in muscles which are particularly affected by FSHD, for example the facial,
                                       abdominal, or paraspinal muscles.5 Recent international FSHD meetings have identified the
                                       need for a biomarker of muscle structure or function for clinical trials.7

                                       Impedance has long been used to assess body composition, because tissues, such as muscle
                                       and fat, have different impedance to current flow. Electrical impedance myography (EIM) is
                                       an adaptation of this technique which uses subthreshold electrical current to determine the
                                       resistance to current flow through a particular muscle. EIM has been shown in preclinical
                                       studies to reflect changes in muscle structure, and techniques have been optimized to be
                                       more sensitive to muscle than underlying skin or fat.8,9 EIM has shown good reliability and




Author Manuscript
                                       relationship to disease severity in diverse neuromuscular diseases, including spinal muscular
                                       atrophy (SMA) and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), and it is responsive to disease
                                       progression in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.10-12

                                       Here we determine the reliability of EIM and its association with known measures of disease
                                       severity in FSHD. In particular, we examine the performance of EIM in muscles particularly
                                       affected in FSHD, including the face, abdominal, and paraspinal musculature.


                        Material and Methods
                                       We performed a prospective cross-sectional observational study of genetically confirmed
                                       and clinically affected FSHD patients at the University of Rochester Medical Center
                                       conducted from 2012 to 2015. The study was approved by the Human Subjects Committee,




Author Manuscript
                                       and written and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

                        Participants
                                       FSHD participants were between 18 and 75 years of age, were clinically affected as
                                       determined by investigator examination, were independently ambulatory, and had genetically
                                       confirmed FSHD type 1 or 2.13,14 Participants were recruited from our neuromuscular
                                       research database or neuromuscular clinic using inclusion criteria reflecting the spectrum

                                      disease severity for a typical, early phase trial. Participants completed a baseline visit, then

                                      local participants (or participants able to extend their stay) returned for a subsequent repeat
                                      visit within 3 weeks of the baseline assessment for reliability testing. Evaluations included
                                      EIM, standardized physical evaluation, manual and quantitative strength testing, and a
                                      battery of standard functional assessments.

                        Outcomes and assessments
                                      The EIM device was obtained from Skulpt, Inc (Boston, MA, Figure 1A,B) and consists of a
                                      laptop computer with a connection to a portable hand-held sensor.15 Evaluators were trained
                                      in technique and standard recording positions during a 1 day training session (Figure 1 C,D).
                                      Pediatric sensors were used for muscles of the face, as they have a smaller surface area, and
                                      are more appropriate for facial muscles.11 The impedance is measured at each frequency by
                                      applying low-intensity electrical current (<1 mA) via surface electrodes embedded in a




Author Manuscript
                                      single hand-held sensor, and measuring the resulting voltage signals using a second set of
                                      surface electrodes embedded in the same sensor, converting them into 3 impedance
                                      parameters, the resistance (R) the reactance (X), and the Phase [arctangent (X/R)]. While the
                                      device records 41 frequencies ranging between 1 kHz and 10 MHz, 50 and 100 kHz were
                                      chosen for this analysis, as they are thought to be more sensitive to biological tissues. The 50
                                      kHz phase is commonly utilized in neuromuscular disease.16 We compared values for phase
                                      and reactance. Three recordings of each muscle were obtained, and the 2 most closely
                                      aligned measurements were averaged (Figure 1B). Recordings were made from the
                                      following bilateral muscle groups: orbicularis oris, deltoid, biceps, triceps, abdominals,
                                      vastus lateralis, tibialis anterior, and thoracic paraspinals. Recordings took approximately 20
                                      minutes for each subject. Summary scores were created by averaging the bilateral arm
                                      muscles, leg muscles, and trunk muscles.




Author Manuscript
                                      The FSHD Clinical Score is a 15-point clinical severity scale composed of scores for
                                      different body regions: face, shoulder, arms, abdomen, and legs (0=unaffected, 15=severely
                                      affected).17

                                      Quantitative myometry was performed using a fixed myometry testing system (QMA,
                                      Gainesville, GA) with standardized positions in 14 bilateral muscle groups: shoulder
                                      abductors, external rotators, elbow flexors and extensors, knee extensors, ankle dorsiflexors,
                                      and handgrips. 18 Manual muscle testing was performed by a trained physical therapist on 28
                                      muscle groups: bilateral shoulder abductors, shoulder external rotators, elbow flexors, wrist
                                      flexors, wrist extensors, hip flexors, hip abductors, knee extensors, hip extensors, knee
                                      flexors, elbow extensors, ankle dorsiflexors, and plantar flexors, plus neck extensor and neck
                                      flexors.18




Author Manuscript
                                      The 6-minute walk test (6MWT) is a test of strength and cardiopulmonary reserve and
                                      determines the distance walked using casual gait in 6 minutes. This test has been shown
                                      previously to have high test-retest reliability in Duchenne/Becker muscular dystrophy, where
                                      it was reliable and responsive to change over time. 19-21 Additional standard functional tests
                                      included: time to ascend 4 stairs; timed supine to sit; and time to don/doff a jacket.22,18

                        Statistical Considerations




Author Manuscript
                                      Distributions for all parameters were tested for normality visually, and by performing the
                                      Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were used to describe all parameters (mean and
                                      standard deviation, median, and quartiles). Test-retest reliability was determined using the
                                      intra-class correlation coefficient based on a mixed effects model, with lower 95%
                                      confidence limits. To ensure technical consistency for reliability testing, outliers with
                                      leverage were identified from the linear regression plots using RStudent and CooksD.
                                      Outliers were removed if visual inspection of the tracing record showed technical
                                      inconsistencies with measurements. Additionally negative values at 10 kHz were used to
                                      identify recordings where probe contact was less than optimal. To determine the relative
                                      sensitivity of phase versus reactance to other measures of FSHD severity, correlation plots of
                                      the Pearson correlation versus EIM parameter at both 50 and 100 kHz were created. Fifty
                                      kHz reactance was determined to be the most sensitive measure and was used for subsequent




Author Manuscript
                                      analysis. The relationships with other known measures of FSHD disease (clinical severity,
                                      strength, and functional measures) were determined using Pearson correlations. All
                                      statistical tests were 2-sided, and P-value cut offs were adjusted for multiple testing (P<0.01
                                      for tables 2 & 4, and P<0.007 for table 3).


                        Results
                                      Thirty-five participants were included, and 18 returned within 3 weeks for test-retest
                                      reliability. Participants were mostly men (Table 1), adults, and moderately affected, but
                                      covering the range of disease severity from mild to moderately-severe. Most participants
                                      were FSHD type 1 (mean D4Z4 fragment size 25.2 kb), except for 2 with SMCHD1
                                      confirmed FSHD type 2.




Author Manuscript
                                      Reliability was good to excellent for major muscle groups (deltoid, biceps, triceps, vastus
                                      lateralis, and tibialis anterior) with ICCs ranging from 0.72-0.99 (Supplementary Table S1,
                                      available online). Reliability for the abdominals and thoracic paraspinals was also acceptable
                                      (ICCs 0.71-0.98). There appeared to be a learning effect for the evaluator for recordings over
                                      the orbicularis oris; there were side-to-side differences in reliability likely due to the order of
                                      testing, with the right side tested first and ICCs <0.5, and the left side showing acceptable
                                      ICCs of 0.84-0.91. This was also likely complicated by technical limitations due to facial
                                      hair, and slightly longer recording times with the smaller pediatric sensors. Overall summary
                                      scores showed higher reliability due to the reduced variability in averaging across multiple
                                      muscles with ICCs for trunk, arm, and leg between 0.89 and 0.98.

                                      Sample tables were made for the biceps, vastus lateralis, and thoracic paraspinal muscles to




Author Manuscript
                                      test the relationship of resistance, reactance, and phase to FSHD severity measures. The
                                      parameter that showed the strongest overall correlation with FSHD disease measures was
                                      reactance at 50 kHz, which was chosen for this study (Table 2). The 50 kHz reactance
                                      showed broad moderate to strong correlations with other FSHD disease measures, which
                                      made sense for the particular muscle being tested. For example the EIM value for the right
                                      vastus lateralis correlated with strength on MMT and QMT, as well as functional tasks like
                                      time to ascend 4 stairs or the 6 minute walk distance (Tables 3 & 4). The summary scores
                                      demonstrated tighter and more significant correlations with clinical severity and functional


                                          Muscle Nerve. Author manuscript; available in PMC 2017 October 01.

                                      measures than the individual muscle scores, likely reflecting the ability of the summary




Author Manuscript
                                      measures to serve as surrogates for overall disease state. The actual relationship between
                                      reactance and disease severity in FSHD was inverse: as severity increases, reactance
                                      decreases (Figure 2).

                                      The relationship of EIM reactance with age was driven by disease duration, which can be
                                      seen by stratifying participants based on their age of diagnosis; a stronger correlation was
                                      seen for participants with longer disease duration (Figure 3A). Evaluating for differences
                                      due to known groups is underpowered for this study; women are known to be diagnosed
                                      later and to be less severely affected per age than men, but no clear difference in reactance or
                                      phase was seen here. Participants with D4Z4 residual fragments <20 kb also tend to have
                                      more severe disease and demonstrated lower reactance values in some muscles (Figure
                                      3B,C).2,23

                        Discussion
                                      EIM of major muscle groups and summary scores were reliable in FSHD, for both phase and
                                      reactance. Reactance appeared to be most sensitive to changes in other measures of FSHD
                                      disease severity, with moderate to strong correlations with other disease measures across a
                                      broad variety of assessments for FSHD. Of particular interest is the reliability and
                                      concordant validity of EIM recordings from muscles normally excluded from FSHD
                                      assessments, such as abdominal and paraspinal muscles. EIM of facial muscles may
                                      ultimately prove to be useful in FSHD if the technical hurdles that affect reliability can be
                                      overcome.

                                      Prior studies in EIM have focused on the phase at 50kHz, as this was felt to be the most
                                      sensitive EIM metric. But this was largely based on the early work in motor neuron diseases,




Author Manuscript
                                      ALS and SMA.10,16,24 Studies designed to assess the impact of different tissue
                                      compartments on EIM measurements suggested reactance was particularly sensitive to
                                      muscle and less sensitive to skin or fat, likely due to the inherent permittivity to current flow
                                      in muscle tissue.8 Resistance is felt to reflect changes in skin and fat. Ultimately the decision
                                      to use 1 electrical parameter or another depends on the reliability of the measurements and
                                      the relationship to a feature of interest in the disease. Phase as a derived measure, which
                                      incorporates both reactance and resistance, may provide more stability. However to the
                                      degree stability in phase is due to resistance it may dilute the association with disease
                                      metrics of interest, because resistance is highly dependent on skin and subcutaneous fat.
                                      Here, although the phase was slightly more reliable in FSHD, there was a strong relationship
                                      with disease metrics when we used reactance. Thus, the increase in validity to disease
                                      metrics of interest should be weighed against any increase in variability of the measurement.




Author Manuscript
                                      In the DMD literature, a couple of approaches to optimize EIM parameters have been used.
                                      In one the relative correlation to skin fold measurements versus correlation with the 6MWT
                                      to pick the frequency which gave the highest correlation to the functional measure, while
                                      minimizing the correlation to skin fold was used.12 We did not optimize based on skin fold
                                      here, but the broad correlations with FSHD disease metrics suggest that 50 kHz reactance
                                      measures a metric of interest to FSHD by providing a value that relates to not only overall


                                          Muscle Nerve. Author manuscript; available in PMC 2017 October 01.
                    Statland et al.                                                                                             Page 6


                                      severity but individual muscle strength and function. Future studies can look at further




Author Manuscript
                                      optimizing this metric, either by evaluating different frequency ranges or ratios of reactance
                                      to phase.25 Many things argue for using the current approach at this stage of development.
                                      We have demonstrated acceptable reliability. Recordings at 50 kHz occur in a stable portion
                                      of the reactance versus frequency curve profile. Reactance already shows promising
                                      correlations to a broad set of FSHD disease measures. Ultimately it is the longitudinal
                                      relationships to change which will need to be used to determine optimized scores. What is
                                      desired from any outcome is reliability, concordant validity, and responsiveness. Caution
                                      must be used when trying to optimize EIM to cross-sectional data, as what is optimal in a
                                      cross sectional association may not prove to be optimal when gauging responsiveness.

                                      EIM should be considered in the context of recent studies of MRI in FSHD. Muscle MRI
                                      studies in FSHD demonstrated fatty infiltration and structural changes, which could be
                                      quantified and followed over time.26,27 These structural changes demonstrate moderate




Author Manuscript
                                      baseline correlations with measures of strength or function. In addition, certain muscles
                                      demonstrated characteristic T2/STIR hyperintensities consistent with inflammation in
                                      otherwise ultrastructurally normal appearing muscle, which raises the possibility of
                                      identifying muscles at risk for future decline.26,28 However, MRI as a clinical outcome tool
                                      has a number of limitations: 1) MRI is not universally available and requires dedicated
                                      facilities; 2) there are significant site-to-site differences in equipment and technique; 3) MRI
                                      can take up to 40 minutes for a single limb; 4) MRI remains an expensive recurring cost; and
                                      5) some patients with FSHD would be excluded from having a MRI due contraindications
                                      (e.g. metal in the body).

                                      The advantages of EIM are readily apparent when collecting the measurements. It is
                                      painless, requires minimal training, and does not require specific expertise in post-




Author Manuscript
                                      processing. Preclinical data have shown good relationships with muscle structure. Muscles
                                      that arenot typically tested can be included, in particular facial, abdominal, and paraspinal
                                      muscles. Lastly, the technology can allow measurements from the home, outside of formal
                                      study visits. The current commercial price for the complete system used here is $18,000, and
                                      there is a $50 per sensor recurring cost. Linear arrays used for tongue measurements may
                                      ultimately prove to be useful for measurements of facial muscles.29 Both MRI and EIM may
                                      prove useful in FSHD. They may be complimentary in evaluating changes in response to
                                      therapeutics and allow better understanding of the natural history of disease in FSHD. For
                                      example, MRI could be used at baseline to stratify patients, and EIM could be used to follow
                                      individual muscles over time.

                                      Limitations to this study include the sample size and inclusion of ambulatory subjects. EIM




Author Manuscript
                                      may also be useful for interventions in non-ambulatory patients, and the ability to reliably
                                      measure more affected muscles is not determined here. Whether EIM ultimately proves
                                      more responsive to disease progression needs to be determined.

                                      EIM is a fast, commercially available, and simple technique for quantifying muscle
                                      composition and function. It expands our ability to measure muscles particularly affected in
                                      FSHD. Future directions will include determining the responsiveness to change in FSHD,
                                      optimizing recordings for clinical trials, and piloting home measurements of EIM.
